Abstract
This document outlines the mathematical architecture of the National Systemic Risk Assessment
Layer (NSRAL) Climate Stress Testing Framework. The core engine incorporates empirical
market data to calculate systemic risk. The architectural pivot reduces reliance on theoretical,
normal-distribution approximations in favor of empirical market realities.
Key structural upgrades include:
•Empirically-Mapped KMV Default Transformation:The engine maps a firm’s
Distance-to-Default (DD) against an empirical Expected Default Frequency (EDF) func-
tion calibrated to true emerging market equity volatility, capturing the leptokurtic tail of
compounding climate events.
•Asymmetric Vine Copula Architecture:The symmetric Student-t Copula has been
replaced with a Clayton Copula to model downside Wrong Way Risk (WWR). This isolates
the correlation between borrower default and physical collateral stranding during stress
periods.
•TCaR Structural Uncertainty Isolation:The Transition Cost-at-Risk (T CaR) mod-
ule evaluates the gap between a firm’s Required Green CAPEX and their Realized Green
CAPEX sourced from BRSR filings.
•Spatial Tensor Interpolation:Core probability and spatial mapping operations have
been refactored to utilize JAX interpolations, supporting Monte Carlo execution.
These enhancements allow NSRAL to produce A-IRB-aligned capital degradation metrics.
1 Macroeconomic Transmission and Scenarios
1.1 NGFS Scenario Architecture
The engine executes multi-decade stress tests anchored by the Network for Greening the Financial
System (NGFS) Phase 4 scenarios. The platform processes trajectories mapping the CMIP6 climate
models, analyzing the impacts of Orderly Transitions versus Disorderly Hot House Worlds.
Under theOrderly Transition (Net Zero 2050)framework, the engine models the impact
of coordinated policy interventions. This scenario introduces a smoothly escalating shadow carbon
price, allowing the macroeconomic environment and structural sectors to adapt gradually while
mitigating physical damage by limiting global warming to 1.5 ◦C.
Conversely, theDisorderly TransitionandHot House World (Current Policies)sce-
narios introduce non-linear shocks. A delayed policy response forces an abrupt implementation
of carbon pricing late in the horizon, introducing transition disruption and stranded asset risks.
Meanwhile, the Hot House World pathway assumes no new climate policies, resulting in unabated
emissions. In this scenario, the engine models lower transition costs but amplifies acute and chronic
physical damages, driven by temperature anomalies and weather events.

3.1 Empirically-Mapped KMV Default Transformation (PD)
The engine builds upon the standard 1974 Vasicek/Merton model by addressing its structural
limitations: the assumption of a single zero-coupon debt barrier and the reliance on a standard
normal distribution, which can underestimate tail risk.
To support Basel A-IRB compliance, the architecture utilizes an Empirically-Mapped KMV
framework. First, the engine defines a dynamic Default Point (DP) that reflects corporate capital
structure, modeling short-term debt and long-term debt dynamics:
DP=Short T erm Debt+ 0.5×Long T erm Debt(13)
Crucially, the architecture incorporates the empirical realities of SME financing in emerging mar-
kets through aStructural Proxy Transfer. While the topological manifold itself is calibrated
using historical equity data from large, listed Indian entities (which possess the observable market
pricing required for Merton inversion), the resulting mapping from Distance-to-Default to Expected
Default Frequency represents a universal mathematical relationship between solvency buffers and
bankruptcy risk.
To construct this core topological manifold, the engine executes a rigorous multi-step empirical
calibration process:
1.Historical Equity Extraction & Inversion: The engine ingests a pre-calculated dataset
of continuous Distance-to-Default (DD) metrics. These metrics are derived by streaming a
decade of historical equity pricing for major listed Indian entities (via ‘yfinance‘) and sub-
jecting them to the numerical Black-Scholes Merton inversion process.
2.Market Moment Extraction: The engine computes the true empirical moments of this
live market solvency data, specifically extracting the structural mean (µ DD) and standard
deviation (σDD) of the Indian corporate universe.
3.Logistic EDF Calibration: Rather than using the standard normal distribution (Φ), the
engine constructs an empirical Expected Default Frequency (EDF) mapping using a Logistic
Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF). Crucially, the decay factor (k) of this logistic curve
is mathematically calibrated to be inversely proportional to the empirical market volatility
(k= 1.0/(0.5×σ DD)). This ensures the default barrier accurately reflects real-world market
distributions.
4.Systemic Contagion & Tail Thickening: To simulate systemic climate stress (the NGFS
Severity domain), the engine mathematically shifts the Distance-to-Default coordinates down-
wards. Furthermore, an explicit probability “tail thickener” is injected for firms near the
default barrier to simulate sudden liquidity freezing. The resulting continuous 3D probability
tensor is the geometric surface depicted in Figure 1.

3.2 Numerical Inversion of Unobservable Asset Parameters (Merton)
The Merton structural model relies on a fundamental insight from corporate finance: holding equity
in a leveraged firm is mathematically analogous to holding a European call option on the firm’s
assets. Because equity holders possess limited liability, their downside is capped. At debt maturity,
if the total value of the firm’s assets (V) falls below the face value of its debt obligations (the
Default Point,DP), the equity holders will simply default and surrender the firm to the creditors
(analogous to letting an out-of-the-money call option expire worthless). Conversely, if the asset
value exceeds the debt, the equity holders pay off the creditors and retain the remaining residual
value (exercising an in-the-money option).

4.2.3 Total Institutional Loss (Loss T otal)
The total loss is an aggregate of five distinct stress vectors sweeping across the institution’s balance
sheet, transcending simple loan defaults:
LossT otal = ∆ECL Inc +Liquidity Draw +M arket Loss +F unding Cost +Op Loss (27)
Where:
•∆ECL Inc: The sum of all incremental Expected Credit Losses generated by borrower defaults
(P ECL Stressed −ECL Base).
•Liquidity Draw: Cash flow losses caused by distressed borrowers maximizing their undrawn
credit lines prior to default.
•M arket Loss: Mark-to-market shock on the bank’s treasury portfolio due to widening climate
credit spreads.
•F unding Cost: The increased premium the bank must pay in wholesale funding markets due
to its own credit rating downgrade.
•Op Loss: Direct physical damage to the bank’s operational infrastructure and branch network,
augmented by transition-related regulatory fines.
